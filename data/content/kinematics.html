Mechanics: Kinematics & Changes

Motion: Relative Rest and Cosmic Dance

What exactly is motion? At its standard definition, motion is merely a change in position relative to a chosen frame of reference over a given span of time. But dig beneath that textbook definition, and you quickly discover a mind-bending cosmic truth: absolute rest is an illusion.

Consider an astronaut sleeping inside the International Space Station. To a crewmate floating nearby, she appears completely stationary—at rest in her sleeping quarters. Yet to a tracking dish in Houston, both astronauts and their 450-ton orbital laboratory are hurtling through the upper atmosphere at 17,500 mph (28,000 km h-1), circling the entire planet once every 90 minutes.

This duality is the bedrock of orbital mechanics. When a SpaceX Crew Dragon capsule approaches the ISS to dock, both spacecraft are traveling around Earth twenty times faster than the speed of sound. Yet, as they align their docking mechanisms, their relative velocity drops to a gentle fraction of an inch per second. In spaceflight, speed is meaningless without asking: speed relative to what?

Zoom out further, and even the solid ground beneath your feet reveals its hidden velocity:

  • Planetary Rotation: Right now, an observer standing on Earth’s equator is spinning around the planet’s axis at roughly 1,000 mph (1,670 km h-1).
  • Solar Orbit: That observer—and the entire Earth—is screaming around the Sun at 67,000 mph (107,000 km h-1).
  • Galactic Transit: The entire solar system orbits the supermassive black hole at the center of the Milky Way at an astounding 490,000 mph (790,000 km h-1).
  • Cosmic Drift: The Milky Way itself is being drawn toward the Andromeda Galaxy and the Great Attractor at more than 1.3 million mph (2.1 million km h-1) relative to the cosmic microwave background radiation.

Even in the deepest abysses of space, true rest remains out of reach. When NASA’s Voyager 1 probe flew past Jupiter, it executed a gravitational slingshot. By diving deep into Jupiter’s gravity well, Voyager stole a tiny fraction of Jupiter’s orbital energy, accelerating the probe to over 38,000 mph relative to the Sun while imperceptibly slowing Jupiter in its orbit. The probe didn't generate motion out of nowhere; it traded momentum in an intricate, multi-body kinetic dance.

From a book resting on a desk to Voyager 1 traversing interstellar dark, every object in the universe is locked in continuous, compounding motion. Nothing is truly fixed, nothing is static, and "standing still" is always a matter of perspective.

The Seamless Coast

How can one explain the seamless, fluid movement of an ice or roller skater who is coasting along without any apparent effort?

When a skater glides seamlessly, they are demonstrating inertia in action. Without the harsh drag of rough surfaces, they continue to move effortlessly forward.

Real-life Example: Contrast sliding a hockey puck on rough concrete (where it stops immediately) versus an air hockey table. On the air hockey table, the puck glides indefinitely until it hits a wall.

Condition for Coasting: Net Force = 0 (Negligible Friction)

Constant (Uniform) Velocity

A straightforward response is to say that a coasting skater travels at a uniform velocity. The word velocityVelocityA change in position over time. It is a vector quantity having both speed and direction.See entire glossary is a distinct technical term, and it requires a clear definition.

For a velocity to be truly uniform, it must remain entirely unchanged over time. This means the object cannot speed up, slow down, or turn.

Real-life Example: A spacecraft drifting in deep space, far away from the gravitational pull of planets or stars, moves in a perfectly straight line at a constant speed indefinitely.

The Birth of Empiricism

In Raphael's famous fresco The School of Athens, we see Plato pointing upwards to the heavens (representing pure, untouchable ideals and mathematics), while his student Aristotle points forward to the physical earth (representing observation and tangible reality).

For centuries, science was dominated by Plato's idea that we can understand the universe purely through logic and thought experiments, without ever getting our hands dirty.

The Experimental Revolution: Modern physics rejected this. Galileo and later Newton realized that logic is not enough—we must test our ideas against reality. If an elegant mathematical theory doesn't match the physical results of an experiment, the theory is wrong. This relentless focus on testing and measuring led to the empirical evidence view that defines modern science today.

The Big Question: How do we measure motion to test our theories? We must define exact physical quantities: position, velocity, and acceleration.

Philosophical Riddle

The Master Variable

Challenge: Can you define all of those core quantities (Position, Velocity, and Acceleration) in terms of just one fundamental variable? What would that variable be, and how would you use it to define all of them?

The Answer: Time ($t$)

The master variable that weaves all motion together is Time. Without time, the universe is just a frozen snapshot. By tracking how things change as time ticks forward, we can define everything else:

  • Position ($s$): Where an object is located at a specific moment in time.
  • Velocity ($v$): The rate at which position changes over time ($v = \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta t}$).
  • Acceleration ($a$): The rate at which velocity changes over time ($a = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$).
  • Force ($F$): A mass multiplied by its acceleration, which is fundamentally tied to how its velocity shifts over time.

Position

PositionPositionThe exact location of an object in space relative to a reference point.See entire glossary serves as the measurement of a body’s precise location in space. Defining a position accurately always demands three distinct pieces of information:

1. Reference Point

A baseline starting location to work from.

2. Distance

The physical separation from the baseline.

3. Direction

The line of orientation relative to the baseline.

If any single one of these elements is missing, determining the object’s actual position becomes impossible. Imagine trying to find buried treasure:

🏴‍☠️ Missing Reference Point

"Walk 10 paces North... but from where?" Without a starting 'X', you are lost.

🏴‍☠️ Missing Distance

"Walk North from the palm tree... but how far?" You could walk forever!

🏴‍☠️ Missing Direction

"Walk 10 paces from the tree..." You'd have to search a massive circle around the tree.

Consequently, a complete position is defined as a specific distance combined with a specific direction measured from an established reference point. The net change in an object's position from its starting point is known as displacementDisplacementA change in position. It is a vector quantity pointing from the initial to the final position.See entire glossary.

Scalar vs Vector Quantities

Scalar Values

Possess only an amount (magnitude). Example: Room temperature or time.

Vector Quantities

Feature both an amount AND a direction. Example: Position or Force.

Crucial Distinction: While they are often used interchangeably in everyday language, in physics, Speed is a scalar quantity (meaning it only has an amount, or magnitude), whereas Velocity is a vector quantity (meaning it has both magnitude and direction). Therefore, speed is simply the magnitude of velocity.

Distance—and thus position—is expressed through linear units. In scientific contexts, the International System of Units (SI) relies on the meter as its standard unit of length. Because standard SI units like the meter may feel less intuitive to general audiences in the United States, everyday units can also be applied for clarity, such as the foot or the mile.

Worked Example

The Mouse's Search for Food

A hungry little mouse leaves its hole in the corner of a kitchen wall (at origin $O: x = 0\text{ m}, y = 0\text{ m}$) and follows a multi-directional route across the kitchen floor searching for food:

  • Leg 1: Scampers $8\text{ meters}$ East toward the refrigerator.
  • Leg 2: Turns and runs $6\text{ meters}$ North along the baseboard.
  • Leg 3: Darts $5\text{ meters}$ West to avoid a sudden noise.
  • Leg 4: Finally moves $2\text{ meters}$ South directly to a piece of cheese on the floor.

Calculate:
(a) The total distance traveled by the mouse.
(b) The magnitude and direction of the mouse’s net displacement from its starting hole to the cheese.

Solution Step-by-Step

Part (a): Total Distance (Scalar Sum)

Distance is a scalar quantity measuring total path length covered regardless of direction: $$\text{Distance } d = d_1 + d_2 + d_3 + d_4 = 8\text{ m} + 6\text{ m} + 5\text{ m} + 2\text{ m} = \mathbf{21\text{ meters}}$$

Part (b): Net Displacement (Vector Change in Position)

1. Calculate net change in horizontal direction ($x$-axis, East positive): $$\Delta x = +8\text{ m (East)} - 5\text{ m (West)} = +\mathbf{3\text{ meters (East)}}$$

2. Calculate net change in vertical direction ($y$-axis, North positive): $$\Delta y = +6\text{ m (North)} - 2\text{ m (South)} = +\mathbf{4\text{ meters (North)}}$$

3. Calculate the magnitude of net displacement using the Pythagorean theorem: $$|\Delta \vec{r}| = \sqrt{(\Delta x)^2 + (\Delta y)^2} = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = \mathbf{5\text{ meters}}$$

4. Determine direction angle $\theta$ North of East: $$\theta = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}\right) = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{4}{3}\right) \approx \mathbf{53.1^\circ \text{ North of East}}$$

Summary: While the mouse ran a total distance of $21\text{ m}$, its straight-line displacement is $5\text{ m}$ at $53.1^\circ$ North of East.

Velocity

When a body is in motion, its velocity is a non-zero value. Velocity quantifies the exact rate at which a body’s position changes as time progresses. Like position, velocity is another vector quantity that demands both a magnitude and a direction:

  • The magnitude (amount) of velocity represents speedSpeedThe rate at which distance is covered. It is the scalar magnitude of velocity.See entire glossary.
  • The direction of velocity represents the heading of movement.
Leaving out either the speed or the travel direction results in an incomplete description of velocity.

1. Same Speed, Different Velocity — The Circular Track

Imagine a runner sprinting around a 400 m oval track at a constant speed of $8\text{ ms}^{-1}$. At every point on the track, the runner's speed (the magnitude of velocity) is identical. Yet the runner's velocity is constantly changing, because the direction of motion is always shifting as they follow the curve.

This is why circular motion always involves acceleration, even when the speed never changes. The velocity vector is tangent to the circle at every point, and since the tangent direction keeps rotating, the velocity keeps changing.

Worked Example

Quarter-Lap Velocity Change

A runner moves at a constant speed of $8\text{ ms}^{-1}$ around a circular track. At point A (top of the track), the velocity is $8\text{ ms}^{-1}$ due East. At point B (right side), the velocity is $8\text{ ms}^{-1}$ due South. Find the magnitude of the change in velocity between A and B.

At A: $\vec{v}_A = 8\hat{x}\text{ ms}^{-1}$ (East)

At B: $\vec{v}_B = -8\hat{y}\text{ ms}^{-1}$ (South)

The change in velocity:

$$\Delta\vec{v} = \vec{v}_B - \vec{v}_A = (-8\hat{y}) - (8\hat{x}) = -8\hat{x} - 8\hat{y}$$ $$|\Delta\vec{v}| = \sqrt{(-8)^2 + (-8)^2} = \sqrt{128} = \mathbf{11.3\text{ ms}^{-1}}$$

Even though the speed stayed at $8\text{ ms}^{-1}$, the velocity changed by $11.3\text{ ms}^{-1}$!

Worked Example

Half-Lap Velocity Change

The same runner completes half a lap. At the starting point, velocity is $8\text{ ms}^{-1}$ due East. At the opposite side, velocity is $8\text{ ms}^{-1}$ due West. Find $|\Delta\vec{v}|$.

$\vec{v}_1 = +8\text{ ms}^{-1}$, $\vec{v}_2 = -8\text{ ms}^{-1}$ (directly opposite)

$$|\Delta\vec{v}| = |(-8) - (+8)| = |-16| = \mathbf{16\text{ ms}^{-1}}$$

The velocity changed by twice the speed — the maximum possible change in one dimension.

Worked Example

Centripetal Acceleration on the Track

A cyclist rides at a constant speed of $12\text{ ms}^{-1}$ around a circular velodrome with radius $r = 25\text{ m}$. Calculate the centripetal acceleration.

Using the centripetal acceleration formula:

$$a_c = \frac{v^2}{r} = \frac{(12)^2}{25} = \frac{144}{25} = \mathbf{5.76\text{ ms}^{-2}}$$

Despite maintaining constant speed, the cyclist accelerates at $5.76\text{ ms}^{-2}$ directed toward the center of the track.

2. The Runner's Paradox — Speed ≠ Velocity

Here is a counterintuitive truth that catches many students off guard: a runner can have a high average speed but zero average velocity. How? Because velocity depends on displacement (the straight-line distance from start to finish), not the total distance traveled.

A runner who completes a full lap around a 400 m track returns to the exact starting point. Their total distance is 400 m, but their displacement is zero — so their average velocity is zero!

$$\text{Average Speed} = \frac{\text{Total Distance}}{\text{Total Time}} \qquad \text{Average Velocity} = \frac{\text{Displacement}}{\text{Total Time}}$$
Worked Example

The Complete Lap

A sprinter runs one complete lap around a 400 m track in $50\text{ s}$. Calculate (a) average speed and (b) average velocity.

(a) Average speed:

$$\bar{s} = \frac{d}{t} = \frac{400}{50} = \mathbf{8\text{ ms}^{-1}}$$

(b) Average velocity:

$$\bar{v} = \frac{\Delta x}{t} = \frac{0}{50} = \mathbf{0\text{ ms}^{-1}}$$

The runner is fast (speed = $8\text{ ms}^{-1}$), but their average velocity is zero because they ended up right where they started.

Worked Example

The Half-Lap

The same sprinter now runs exactly half a lap (200 m along the track) in $25\text{ s}$. The track has a diameter of approximately $127\text{ m}$. Calculate (a) average speed and (b) average velocity.

(a) Average speed:

$$\bar{s} = \frac{200}{25} = \mathbf{8\text{ ms}^{-1}}$$

(b) Average velocity: The displacement is the straight-line distance across the track (the diameter):

$$\bar{v} = \frac{127}{25} = \mathbf{5.08\text{ ms}^{-1}}$$

Now the average velocity is non-zero, because the runner didn't return to the start.

Worked Example

Back-and-Forth Walk

A student walks $80\text{ m}$ North in $40\text{ s}$, then immediately turns around and walks $30\text{ m}$ South in $20\text{ s}$. Find (a) average speed and (b) average velocity.

(a) Average speed:

$$\bar{s} = \frac{80 + 30}{40 + 20} = \frac{110}{60} = \mathbf{1.83\text{ ms}^{-1}}$$

(b) Average velocity (taking North as positive):

$$\bar{v} = \frac{80 - 30}{60} = \frac{50}{60} = \mathbf{0.83\text{ ms}^{-1}\text{ [North]}}$$

The average speed is more than double the average velocity, because the student backtracked.

3. The ISS in Orbit — Constant Speed, Perpetual Acceleration

The International Space Station orbits Earth at a nearly constant speed of $28{,}000\text{ kmh}^{-1}$ ($7{,}780\text{ ms}^{-1}$). Yet, remarkably, it is always accelerating. How is this possible? Because its velocity direction is continuously changing as it follows its curved orbital path. The ISS is in constant free fall toward Earth, but its enormous tangential speed means it keeps "missing" the surface.

This acceleration is directed toward Earth's center (centripetal) and is provided entirely by gravity. It is the same $g$ that pulls an apple from a tree — just weakened slightly at orbital altitude.

Worked Example

Orbital Period of the ISS

The ISS orbits at an altitude of approximately $408\text{ km}$ above Earth's surface. Earth's radius is $6{,}371\text{ km}$. Given the ISS's speed of $7{,}780\text{ ms}^{-1}$, calculate the time for one complete orbit.

Orbital radius: $r = 6{,}371 + 408 = 6{,}779\text{ km} = 6.779 \times 10^6\text{ m}$

Circumference of orbit:

$$C = 2\pi r = 2\pi(6.779 \times 10^6) = 4.259 \times 10^7\text{ m}$$

Orbital period:

$$T = \frac{C}{v} = \frac{4.259 \times 10^7}{7{,}780} = 5{,}474\text{ s} = \mathbf{91.2\text{ minutes}}$$

The ISS completes a full orbit in about 90 minutes — seeing 16 sunrises every day!

Worked Example

Centripetal Acceleration of the ISS

Using the values from the previous Worked Example, calculate the centripetal acceleration of the ISS. Compare it to $g = 9.81\text{ ms}^{-2}$.

$$a_c = \frac{v^2}{r} = \frac{(7{,}780)^2}{6.779 \times 10^6} = \frac{6.053 \times 10^7}{6.779 \times 10^6} = \mathbf{8.93\text{ ms}^{-2}}$$

Comparing to surface gravity:

$$\frac{a_c}{g} = \frac{8.93}{9.81} = 0.91$$

The gravitational pull at the ISS altitude is still 91% of what we feel on Earth's surface! Astronauts feel "weightless" not because gravity disappears, but because they are in continuous free fall.

Worked Example

Velocity Change Over a Quarter Orbit

The ISS moves at $7{,}780\text{ ms}^{-1}$. Calculate the magnitude of the change in velocity over a quarter of its orbit (from heading East to heading South).

At position 1: $\vec{v}_1 = 7{,}780\hat{x}\text{ ms}^{-1}$ (East)

At position 2: $\vec{v}_2 = -7{,}780\hat{y}\text{ ms}^{-1}$ (South)

$$|\Delta\vec{v}| = \sqrt{(7{,}780)^2 + (7{,}780)^2} = 7{,}780\sqrt{2} = \mathbf{11{,}003\text{ ms}^{-1}}$$

Converting: $11{,}003\text{ ms}^{-1} = 39{,}611\text{ kmh}^{-1}$

In just ~23 minutes (quarter orbit), the ISS's velocity changes by over 39,000 km h-1 — faster than most bullets — even though its speed never changed at all.

Force & Newton's First Law

The third key physical quantity is forceForceA push or a pull exerted on an object. A vector quantity causing acceleration.See entire glossary, which in physics serves as the technical term for any push or pull. Force is also a vector quantity:

  • The magnitude corresponds to how hard the push or pull is applied.
  • The direction corresponds to the orientation of that push or pull (e.g., pushing a book directly away from someone).

1 Newton ($\text{N}$)

Roughly the downward force of holding a small apple in your hand.

~3 Newtons

The force exerted by holding a larger piece of fruit, like a grapefruit.

4.5 Newtons

Approximately equal to 1 pound-force (the common unit in the US).

With these physical definitions in place, Newton's first law of motion can be rephrased with much greater precision:

Replacing vague terms like "external influences" with "forces" yields: An object free of forces moves in a straight line, covering equal distances in equal time intervals.

Breaking down the motion components reveals that moving in a straight line implies a constant direction, while covering equal distances in equal time intervals implies a constant speed.

Combining a fixed direction with a fixed speed yields a constant velocity.

Thus, the refined formulation of Newton's First Law of Motion states:

An object that experiences no net external forces moves at a constant velocity.

A coasting skater experiencing zero net force is governed directly by this principle, traveling along with unchanged speed and direction. While completely shielding an object from every outside force is practically impossible in the real world, it is simple to have opposing forces balance each other out so the net external force totals zero. For instance, if two equal pushes are applied to a skater from opposite sides (one pushing right, the other pushing left with identical strength), the two forces cancel completely. With no remaining net force, the skater acts as an inertial object and continues coasting at a constant velocity.

Motion Graphs

Graphical representations provide a powerful visual tool for analyzing an object's motion over time. There are three primary types of motion graphs you must be familiar with:

Displacement-Time (s-t)

The slope (gradient) of this graph represents the velocity of the object. A horizontal line means the object is stationary.

Velocity-Time (v-t)

The slope represents acceleration. The area under the curve represents the total displacement.

Acceleration-Time (a-t)

A horizontal line indicates constant acceleration. The area under the curve represents the change in velocity.

Worked Example

Interpreting a Journey Graph

A car's motion is recorded on a velocity-time graph for a $10$-second interval. It starts from rest, accelerates uniformly to $20\text{ ms}^{-1}$ over the first $4\text{ s}$, travels at a constant velocity for $3\text{ s}$, and then uniformly brakes to a halt over the final $3\text{ s}$.

Determine:
(a) The acceleration of the car during the first $4\text{ s}$.
(b) The total distance traveled by the car over the $10\text{ s}$ journey.

Solution Step-by-Step

Part (a): Acceleration from $t=0$ to $t=4\text{ s}$

The acceleration is given by the slope (gradient) of the velocity-time graph: $$a = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = \frac{v_f - v_i}{t_f - t_i} = \frac{20 - 0}{4 - 0} = \mathbf{5\text{ ms}^{-2}}$$

Part (b): Total Distance

The total distance traveled is equal to the area under the velocity-time graph, which forms a trapezium (or can be split into a triangle, rectangle, and triangle).

Using the area of a trapezium formula: $Area = \frac{1}{2}(a + b)h$ where $a$ is the top parallel side (the constant velocity duration, $3\text{ s}$), $b$ is the bottom parallel side (the total duration, $10\text{ s}$), and $h$ is the height (max velocity, $20\text{ ms}^{-1}$).

$$\text{Distance} = \frac{1}{2}(3 + 10) \times 20 = \frac{1}{2}(13) \times 20 = 13 \times 10 = \mathbf{130\text{ meters}}$$
Summary: The car accelerates at $5\text{ ms}^{-2}$ initially and covers a total distance of $130\text{ m}$ during the trip.

Introduction to Projectile Motion

Projectile motion occurs when an object is launched into the air and is subject only to the acceleration of gravity. The key to understanding projectile motion is recognizing that the vertical and horizontal components of velocity are completely independent from each other.

Breaking Down Velocity

When an object is launched at an angle, its initial resultant velocity ($v$) must be split into two separate parts:

  • Horizontal Velocity ($v_x$): Moves the object forward. Since gravity only acts downwards, $v_x$ remains constant throughout the flight (ignoring air resistance).
  • Vertical Velocity ($v_y$): Moves the object up and down. This component is affected by gravity, acting as free fall.

Using basic trigonometry (sine and cosine), we can calculate these components from the launch speed $v$ and launch angle $\theta$:

$v_x = v \cdot \cos(\theta)$ $v_y = v \cdot \sin(\theta)$

Empirical Evidence: Video Tracking Analysis

When we use tracking software on a video of an object dropped from a hand moving horizontally, we can mathematically prove the independence of its horizontal and vertical components. By recording the motion and plotting position and velocity against time, the data reveals two completely distinct behaviors occurring simultaneously.

The object's actual path is a combination of horizontal range and vertical drop.

By splitting the video data into horizontal ($x$) and vertical ($y$) components, the resulting graphs look like this:

  • Horizontal Motion (Left side): The position ($x$) graph is a straight diagonal line, and the velocity ($v_x$) graph is flat. This proves that horizontal velocity is constant (acceleration = 0).
  • Vertical Motion (Right side): The position ($y$) graph curves downwards parabolically, and the velocity ($v_y$) graph is a straight diagonal line. This proves that vertical acceleration is constant (free fall due to gravity).
Worked Example

Modeling Projectile Motion

An object is dropped at a height from the hand of a person who is moving horizontally to the left. To understand the initial velocity of the object, we look at the displacement of the object horizontally and vertically.

Calculate:
Using the principle that horizontal and vertical parts are split, determine the equations for:
(a) The vertical displacement (Height).
(b) The horizontal displacement (Range).

Solution Step-by-Step

Part (a): Vertical Displacement (Height)

We can treat the vertical part as a free fall. The initial vertical velocity is $u_y = 0$.
Using the equation of motion $s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2$:
$$s_y = 0 \cdot t + \frac{1}{2}at^2 \implies \mathbf{s_y = \frac{1}{2}at^2}$$ (Where $a$ is the downward acceleration due to gravity, $g$).

Part (b): Horizontal Displacement (Range)

The horizontal velocity $u_x$ is constant because there is no acceleration in the horizontal direction.
Using the equation for constant velocity $s = ut$:
$$\mathbf{s_x = u_xt}$$

Worked Example

Projectile Motion

Problem: A skateboarder rolls off a horizontal ramp at a speed of $5.0\text{ ms}^{-1}$. The ramp is $1.2\text{ m}$ above the ground. How long does it take for the skateboarder to hit the ground? (Use $g = 9.8\text{ ms}^{-2}$)

Knowns:
Initial vertical velocity, $u_y = 0\text{ ms}^{-1}$ (horizontal launch)
Vertical displacement, $s_y = -1.2\text{ m}$ (downward)
Acceleration, $a_y = -9.8\text{ ms}^{-2}$

Equation: Use $s_y = u_y t + \frac{1}{2}a_y t^2$

Calculation:
$-1.2 = 0 + \frac{1}{2}(-9.8)t^2$
$t = \sqrt{\frac{1.2}{4.9}} \approx 0.49\text{ s}$

Answer: The skateboarder is in the air for $0.49\text{ s}$.

Worked Example

Projectile Motion

Problem: Using the time of flight from the previous example ($0.49\text{ s}$), how far horizontally does the skateboarder travel before landing?

Knowns:
Horizontal velocity, $u_x = 5.0\text{ ms}^{-1}$ (constant)
Time, $t = 0.49\text{ s}$

Equation: Use $s_x = u_x t$ (since horizontal acceleration $a_x = 0$)

Calculation:
$s_x = (5.0)(0.49)$
$s_x = 2.45\text{ m}$

Answer: The horizontal range is $2.45\text{ m}$.

Worked Example

Projectile Motion

Problem: A skater launches off a jump at an angle of $30^{\circ}$ above the horizontal with a speed of $10.0\text{ ms}^{-1}$. Calculate the maximum height they reach above the launch point.

Step 1: Find initial vertical velocity.
$u_y = u \sin(\theta) = 10.0 \sin(30^{\circ}) = 5.0\text{ ms}^{-1}$

Step 2: Use kinematic equation for max height.
At max height, final vertical velocity $v_y = 0$.
Use $v_y^2 = u_y^2 + 2a_y s_y$

Step 3: Calculate.
$0 = (5.0)^2 + 2(-9.8)s_y$
$0 = 25 - 19.6 s_y$
$s_y = \frac{25}{19.6} \approx 1.28\text{ m}$

Answer: The maximum height reached is $1.28\text{ m}$.
