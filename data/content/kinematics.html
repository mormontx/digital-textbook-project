Kinematics

Kinematics is the study of motion without considering the forces that cause it. We describe motion using quantities like displacement, velocity, and acceleration.

Scalars and Vectors

Physical quantities can be classified as scalars (magnitude only) or vectors (magnitude and direction).

Examples

Scalars: distance, speed, time, mass, energy

Vectors: displacement, velocity, acceleration, force

Position, Displacement and Distance

The position of an object tells us where it is located in space. Displacement is the change in position—a vector from initial to final position. Distance is the total path length traveled (scalar).

$$\vec{\Delta x} = \vec{x}_f - \vec{x}_i$$

Velocity and Speed

Velocity is the rate of change of displacement (vector). Speed is the rate of change of distance (scalar).

$$\vec{v} = \frac{d\vec{x}}{dt}$$

Average velocity:

$$\vec{v}_{avg} = \frac{\Delta \vec{x}}{\Delta t}$$

Instantaneous velocity is the velocity at a specific moment—the limit of average velocity as Δt approaches zero.

Acceleration

Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity:

$$\vec{a} = \frac{d\vec{v}}{dt} = \frac{d^2\vec{x}}{dt^2}$$

Equations of Motion (SUVAT)

For uniformly accelerated motion (constant acceleration):

$$s = \frac{u + v}{2} \cdot t$$ $$v = u + at$$ $$s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2$$ $$v^2 = u^2 + 2as$$

Projectile Motion

A projectile is any object thrown, launched, or dropped into the air that moves under the influence of gravity alone (neglecting air resistance in first-approximation models). The path followed by a projectile is called its trajectory, which forms a parabola.

The Principle of Independence

The fundamental secret of projectile motion is that horizontal motion and vertical motion are completely independent of one another. The constant force of gravity pulls only downward, affecting only the vertical velocity ($a_y = -g$). Meanwhile, there is no horizontal force acting on the projectile, meaning its horizontal velocity component remains constant throughout the flight ($a_x = 0$).

Mathematical Analysis

Let a projectile be launched with an initial velocity $u$ at an angle $\theta$ relative to the horizontal. We resolve the initial velocity vector into two orthogonal components:

$$u_x = u \cos\theta$$ $$u_y = u \sin\theta$$

1. Horizontal Components (Constant Velocity)

Since horizontal acceleration $a_x = 0$:

$$v_x(t) = u \cos\theta$$ $$x(t) = (u \cos\theta) \cdot t$$

2. Vertical Components (Constant Acceleration)

With constant vertical acceleration under gravity ($a_y = -g$ pointing downwards):

$$v_y(t) = u \sin\theta - g \cdot t$$ $$y(t) = (u \sin\theta) \cdot t - \frac{1}{2}g \cdot t^2$$ $$v_y^2 = (u \sin\theta)^2 - 2g \cdot y$$

Key Derivations

A. Time of Flight ($T_{\text{flight}}$):
The total time the projectile takes to return to the launch height (where $y = 0$):

$$0 = (u \sin\theta) T - \frac{1}{2}g T^2 \implies T_{\text{flight}} = \frac{2 u \sin\theta}{g}$$

B. Maximum Altitude ($H_{\text{max}}$):
At the highest point, the vertical velocity becomes zero ($v_y = 0$):

$$0 = (u \sin\theta)^2 - 2g H_{\text{max}} \implies H_{\text{max}} = \frac{u^2 \sin^2\theta}{2g}$$

C. Horizontal Range ($R$):
The total horizontal distance traveled during the time of flight:

$$R = (u \cos\theta) \cdot T_{\text{flight}} = (u \cos\theta) \cdot \text{ }\left(\frac{2 u \sin\theta}{g}\right) = \frac{u^2 (2 \sin\theta \cos\theta)}{g} \implies R = \frac{u^2 \sin(2\theta)}{g}$$
Range Insights

1. Maximum Range occurs when $\sin(2\theta)$ is maximized. This happens at $2\theta = 90^\circ$, which implies $\theta = 45^\circ$.

2. Complementary Angles (angles adding up to $90^\circ$, like $30^\circ$ and $60^\circ$) yield the exact same horizontal range since $\sin(2(90^\circ-\theta)) = \sin(180^\circ - 2\theta) = \sin(2\theta)$.

Effect of Fluid Resistance

Reduces range, max height, and time of flight. Trajectory becomes asymmetric; terminal speed is reached.
