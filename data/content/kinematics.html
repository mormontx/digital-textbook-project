When considering position, velocity, and time, which concept should be established first?

How can one explain the seamless, fluid movement of an ice or roller skater who is coasting along? A straightforward response is to say that a coasting skater travels at a uniform velocity. The word velocityVelocityA change in position over time. It is a vector quantity having both speed and direction.See entire glossary is a distinct technical term, and it requires a clear definition. Shortly, Newton’s first law can be restated using more exact, elevated terminology. However, doing so requires first introducing three fundamental physical quantities connected with motion: position, velocity, and force.

Position

PositionPositionThe exact location of an object in space relative to a reference point.See entire glossary serves as the measurement of a body’s precise location in space. For instance, a speaker might state that their position is approximately two meters in front of a specific jar. Defining a position accurately always demands three distinct pieces of information:

  • Reference point: A baseline starting location to work from (such as the jar).
  • Distance: The physical separation from that baseline (such as roughly six and a half feet, or two meters).
  • Direction: The line of orientation relative to that baseline (such as "in front of").

If any single one of these three elements—reference point, distance, or direction—is missing, determining the object’s actual position becomes impossible:

  • Missing the reference point: If the reference point (the bottle) is removed while stating an offset of six feet, the actual location remains unknown.
  • Missing the distance: If the direction is known ("in front of") but the separation is omitted, the location could be nearby or far away without any way to specify.
  • Missing the direction: If the distance is set at six and a half feet (two meters) but no direction is given, the location could sit anywhere along a full circle at that radius around the point.

Consequently, a complete position is defined as a specific distance combined with a specific direction measured from an established reference point. The net change in an object's position from its starting point is known as displacementDisplacementA change in position. It is a vector quantity pointing from the initial to the final position.See entire glossary.

Physical properties that feature both a numerical amount and a direction belong to a category known as vector quantitiesVector QuantitiesPhysical properties that feature both a numerical amount (magnitude) and a direction.See entire glossary. Many familiar physical quantities possess only an amount (scalar valuesScalar ValuesPhysical quantities that possess only an amount (magnitude), without direction.See entire glossary), such as room temperature, the total duration of a concert, or the screen size of a laptop. However, numerous crucial physical concepts incorporate both magnitude and spatial orientation; omitting the directional element of a vector quantity leaves the definition incomplete.

Distance—and thus position—is expressed through linear units. In scientific contexts, the International System of Units (SI) relies on the meter as its standard unit of length. Because standard SI units like the meter may feel less intuitive to general audiences in the United States, everyday units can also be applied for clarity, such as the foot or the mile.

Velocity

When a body is in motion, its velocity is a non-zero value. Velocity quantifies the exact rate at which a body’s position changes as time progresses. Like position, velocity is another vector quantity that demands both a magnitude and a direction:

  • The magnitude (amount) of velocity represents speedSpeedThe rate at which distance is covered. It is the scalar magnitude of velocity.See entire glossary.
  • The direction of velocity represents the heading of movement.

For example, a person standing still has a velocity of zero. If that person starts moving toward an observer's left at roughly half a foot per second, they possess a specific leftward velocity. If they then walk toward the right at that same half-a-foot-per-second speed, their velocity has changed—even though their speed remains identical—because the heading is now reversed. Leaving out either the speed or the travel direction results in an incomplete description of velocity.

In terms of measurement, velocity is expressed as distance divided by time:

Force & Newton's First Law

The third key physical quantity is forceForceA push or a pull exerted on an object. A vector quantity causing acceleration.See entire glossary, which in physics serves as the technical term for any push or pull. Force is also a vector quantity:

  • The magnitude corresponds to how hard the push or pull is applied.
  • The direction corresponds to the orientation of that push or pull (e.g., pushing a book directly away from someone).

The standard SI unit for measuring force is the Newton ($\text{N}$). As a visual reference, holding a small apple in your hand exerts roughly one Newton of downward force; a larger piece of fruit might exert closer to three Newtons. In the United States, force is commonly measured in pounds (specifically, pound-force), where one pound-force equals approximately $4.5\text{ Newtons}$.

With these physical definitions in place, Newton's first law of motion can be rephrased with much greater precision:

Replacing vague terms like "external influences" with "forces" yields: An object free of forces moves in a straight line, covering equal distances in equal time intervals.

Breaking down the motion components reveals that moving in a straight line implies a constant direction, while covering equal distances in equal time intervals implies a constant speed.

Combining a fixed direction with a fixed speed yields a constant velocity.

Thus, the refined formulation of Newton's First Law of Motion states:

An object that experiences no net external forces moves at a constant velocity.

A coasting skater experiencing zero net force is governed directly by this principle, traveling along with unchanged speed and direction. While completely shielding an object from every outside force is practically impossible in the real world, it is simple to have opposing forces balance each other out so the net external force totals zero. For instance, if two equal pushes are applied to a skater from opposite sides (one pushing right, the other pushing left with identical strength), the two forces cancel completely. With no remaining net force, the skater acts as an inertial object and continues coasting at a constant velocity.

Acceleration

How does a skater initiate movement, bring themselves to a stop, or change direction? They do so because an applied force acts upon them, bringing about accelerationAccelerationThe rate at which an object's velocity changes over time.See entire glossary.

Acceleration is another fundamental vector quantity that describes how an object's velocity evolves over time. Unlike simple speed, acceleration can be subtle and difficult to spot at a glance; it requires careful observation. Because it is a vector, acceleration possesses both a magnitude (how rapidly velocity is altering) and a direction (where that change is oriented).

Visualizing Acceleration

Consider a person standing completely motionless at zero velocity:

  • As long as they remain stationary, their velocity stays zero over time, meaning their acceleration is zero.
  • If they begin gaining speed toward the right, moving faster and faster from a slow start, their velocity is continuously changing toward the right. This changing rate of motion is the acceleration itself.
  • If they instead burst rapidly toward the left, they undergo a sharp acceleration directed to the left.

When acceleration acts in the exact same direction as the current velocity, the overall speed increases over time. This is precisely how a skater starts moving from rest: starting at zero velocity, they select a direction, accelerate along that line, and continually build up speed.

Speeding Up, Stopping, and Turning

The relative orientation between an object's current velocity vector and its acceleration vector dictates how its movement evolves:

  • Accelerating parallel to velocity (Speeding Up): When acceleration points in the same direction as the existing path of motion, the object gains speed.
  • Accelerating opposite to velocity (Deceleration): When acceleration points in the direction directly opposite to the current motion, the object slows down.

    Example: If an object moves toward the right while accelerating toward the left, its rightward speed continually drops until it comes to a brief, momentary standstill. If the leftward acceleration persists beyond that stop, the object reverses course and begins picking up speed toward the left.

The term decelerationDecelerationA specific case of acceleration where the force and acceleration vectors oppose the motion vector, causing the object to slow down.See entire glossary is simply a specific case of acceleration where the force and acceleration vectors oppose the motion vector.

  • Accelerating sideways to velocity (Changing Direction): Rather than pushing along or directly against the line of travel, acceleration can also be applied laterally to the side of the current path, bending the trajectory without necessarily changing the overall speed.

Equations of motion

We can use equations to determine the motion of uniformly accelerated objects. These equations are often known as the 'suvat' equations.

First, we need to define some quantities. Table 4 shows each quantity, its symbol and its unit.

Table 4. Quantities in suvat equations.
Quantity Symbol Unit
Displacement $s$ m
Initial velocity $u$ $\text{m s}^{-1}$
Final velocity $v$ $\text{m s}^{-1}$
Acceleration $a$ $\text{m s}^{-2}$
Time $t$ s

The core kinematic equations are:

$$v = u + at, \quad s = \left(\frac{u+v}{2}\right)t, \quad s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2, \quad v^2 = u^2 + 2as$$

A skateboarder coasting at $6.0\text{ m s}^{-1}$ encounters friction causing deceleration at $-0.80\text{ m s}^{-2}$. Calculate how far they will roll before coming to a stop.
